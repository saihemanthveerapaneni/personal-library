Abstract
Developmental	stuttering	is	a	childhood-onset	fluency	disorder	characterized	by	repetitions,	prolongations,
and	blocks	in	speech.	Although	many	preschool	children	who	begin	stuttering	recover	naturally,	a	smaller
group	develops	persistent	stuttering	that	continues	into	adolescence	and	adulthood.	Early	identification	of
children	at	elevated	risk	could	help	speech-language	pathologists	prioritize	assessment,	monitoring,	parent
counselling,	and	intervention.
This	project	proposes	an	AI-based	risk-assessment	system	that	analyzes	a	short	natural	speech	recording
from	a	child	and	estimates	the	probability	that	early	childhood	stuttering	will	persist	rather	than	recover.	The
proposed	system	uses	automatically	extracted	acoustic,	temporal,	disfluency,	linguistic,	and	optional	clinical-
history	features.	It	is	designed	as	a	clinical	decision-support	tool,	not	a	diagnostic	tool,	and	provides	an
interpretable	probabilistic	output	such	as	a	persistence-risk	score	and	the	features	that	contributed	to	that
estimate.
The	proposal	is	grounded	in	longitudinal	stuttering	research	showing	that	factors	including	time	since	onset,
family	history,	stuttering	characteristics,	language	ability,	speech	sound	skills,	and	severity	are	associated
with	persistence.	Existing	AI	research	primarily	focuses	on	detecting	stuttering	events;	long-term	prediction
of	persistence	from	early	speech	is	a	less	developed	but	clinically	meaningful	research	gap.	Recent
systematic-review	evidence	also	suggests	that	automatic	stuttering	detection	has	promising	reported
performance	but	substantial	heterogeneity	across	studies,	supporting	the	need	for	careful,	externally
validated	research.	(
pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
)

Introduction
Stuttering	is	a	neurodevelopmental	speech-fluency	disorder	involving	involuntary	disruptions	such	as	sound
or	syllable	repetitions,	prolongations,	and	speech	blocks.	It	commonly	emerges	in	early	childhood,	often
between	ages	two	and	five.	A	substantial	proportion	of	children	recover	naturally,	while	others	develop
persistent	stuttering	that	can	affect	communication,	educational	participation,	confidence,	and	quality	of	life.
(
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov
)
The	major	clinical	challenge	is	not	simply	identifying	whether	a	child	stutters	at	the	time	of	assessment.	The
more	important	question	is	whether	the	child’s	early	stuttering	is	likely	to	persist	or	recover.	Existing	clinical
research	has	identified	predictors	such	as	family	history,	age	and	duration	of	onset,	stuttering	frequency	and
severity,	speech	sound	ability,	language	development,	and	certain	disfluency	patterns.	(
rcslt.org
)
This	project	investigates	whether	machine-learning	methods	can	automatically	extract	useful	predictive
characteristics	from	child	speech	recordings	and	combine	them	with	optional	clinical	information	to	estimate
persistence	risk.
Problem	Statement
Speech-language	pathologists	often	need	to	make	early	decisions	about	whether	a	child	should	receive
immediate	treatment,	monitoring,	parent	counselling,	or	referral	for	comprehensive	assessment.	However,
persistence	versus	recovery	cannot	be	determined	with	certainty	from	a	single	speech	recording.
Existing	AI	tools	generally	focus	on:
Detecting	stuttering	events	in	an	audio	clip.

Identifying	repetitions,	prolongations,	or	blocks.
Supporting	therapy	exercises.
Improving	fluency	or	voice	reconstruction.
There	is	a	gap	in	systems	designed	specifically	to	answer:
Given	a	child’s	current	speech	characteristics,	what	is	the	estimated	future	risk	that	developmental
stuttering	will	persist	rather	than	recover?
The	project	therefore	proposes	an	interpretable	machine-learning	model	for	
early	persistence-risk
prediction
,	rather	than	a	simple	“stammering	detector.”
Project	Objective
To	develop	and	evaluate	a	machine-learning	system	that	predicts	the	probability	of	persistent	childhood
stuttering	from	early	speech	characteristics	and	optional	clinical-history	information.
Research	Question
Can	machine-learning	models	trained	on	early	childhood	speech	characteristics	predict	whether
developmental	stuttering	will	persist	or	recover?
Research	Hypothesis
Speech	characteristics	observed	during	early	childhood	contain	predictive	information	about	whether
developmental	stuttering	will	persist	or	recover.	A	machine-learning	model	combining	acoustic,	temporal,
disfluency,	linguistic,	and	clinical-history	features	can	provide	clinically	useful	persistence-risk	estimates.
Specific	Objectives
Build	an	audio-processing	pipeline	for	extracting	acoustic	and	timing	features	from	child	speech.
Detect	and	quantify	stuttering-like	disfluencies,	including	repetitions,	prolongations,	blocks,	and	abnormal
pauses.
Develop	an	interpretable	baseline	model	using	Logistic	Regression,	Random	Forest,	or	XGBoost.
Compare	audio-only,	audio-plus-transcript,	and	multimodal	models.
Evaluate	the	model	using	child-level	train/test	splits	to	prevent	speaker	leakage.
Measure	sensitivity,	specificity,	ROC-AUC,	precision,	recall,	F1-score,	and	probability	calibration.
Produce	interpretable	results	showing	the	main	factors	contributing	to	each	risk	estimate.
Design	a	prototype	web	interface	for	clinicians	or	researchers.

Scientific	Rationale
Longitudinal	research	indicates	that	childhood	stuttering	is	not	a	uniform	condition.	Some	children	recover
spontaneously,	whereas	others	continue	to	stutter.	Research	has	associated	persistence	with	demographic,
familial,	language,	speech-motor,	and	fluency-related	factors.	(
rcslt.org
)
A	recent	study	reported	that	lower	sentence	comprehension	relative	to	word	comprehension	in	preschool
children	who	stutter	was	associated	with	persistent	stuttering	and	stuttering	severity	at	school	age.	Research
also	continues	to	examine	whether	characteristics	such	as	stuttering	on	content	versus	function	words	may
predict	persistence	in	young	children.	(
tandfonline.com
)
Cumulative-risk	approaches	have	combined	variables	such	as	time	since	onset,	speech	sound	skills,
expressive	language,	and	stuttering	severity.	The	study	discussed	in	the	original	project	context	reported
93%	sensitivity	and	65%	specificity	for	children	with	at	least	two	risk	factors	in	its	study	sample.	This
supports	the	central	idea	that	persistence	is	better	approached	as	a	multi-factor	risk-estimation	problem	than
as	a	single-feature	detection	task.

Train/Test	Splitting	Strategy
A	major	methodological	risk	is	data	leakage.
Incorrect	Split
Child	A	-	Recording	1	->	Training	set
Child	A	-	Recording	2	->	Test	set
The	model	may	recognize	the	child’s	voice	rather	than	generalizable	speech	characteristics.
Correct	Split
Children	A,	B,	C,	D	->	Training	set
Children	E,	F,	G,	H	->	Test	set
All	recordings	from	one	child	must	remain	in	the	same	split.	Use	
GroupKFold
	or

Why	Calibration	Matters
A	system	that	says	“70%	risk”	should	ideally	correspond	to	approximately	70%	of	comparable	children	having
persistent	outcomes.	A	model	may	classify	correctly	but	still	provide	poorly	calibrated	probabilities,	which	is
unsafe	for	clinical	decision	support.
Ethical	Considerations
Decision	Support,	Not	Diagnosis
The	system	must	not	state	that	a	child	“will	definitely	have	persistent	stuttering.”	It	should	report	risk
estimates	with	uncertainty	and	recommend	professional	assessment	when	needed.
Privacy
Child	speech	is	highly	sensitive	data.	Audio	should	be	encrypted	during	storage	and	transfer,	with	access
control,	consent	procedures,	and	de-identification.
Bias	and	Fairness
Model	performance	should	be	analyzed	across:
Age	groups.
Sex.
Language	and	dialect.
Socioeconomic	setting.
Recording-device	quality.
Regional	accent,	including	Indian-English	and	Tamil-influenced	English	where	applicable.
Parent	Communication
Outputs	must	avoid	causing	unnecessary	anxiety.	Since	many	children	recover	naturally,	the	user	interface
should	clearly	explain	that	risk	scores	are	not	diagnoses.
Clinical	Governance
A	qualified	speech-language	pathologist	must	remain	responsible	for	interpretation,	referral,	and	treatment
decisions.

Limitations	and	Risks
Longitudinal	pediatric	datasets	are	small	and	often	restricted.
Many	public	datasets	focus	on	adults,	podcasts,	or	event-level	detection	rather	than	child	persistence
outcomes.
Labels	may	vary	across	studies	depending	on	the	clinical	definition	of	recovery	and	persistence.
Child	speech	differs	substantially	by	age,	accent,	language,	and	recording	conditions.
The	number	of	recordings	may	be	large,	but	the	number	of	unique	children	may	be	small.
There	is	a	high	risk	of	overfitting	unless	validation	is	performed	by	child.
Models	may	not	generalize	from	English-speaking	datasets	to	Indian	children	without	local	validation.
Clinical	usefulness	depends	on	interpretability,	calibration,	and	integration	with	SLP	judgment.
Conclusion
The	proposed	project	is	a	strong	research	and	healthcare-AI	idea	because	it	addresses	a	real	clinical	decision
problem:	estimating	the	risk	that	early	childhood	stuttering	will	persist	rather	than	recover.	Existing
longitudinal	research	supports	the	presence	of	measurable	persistence-related	factors,	while	emerging	AI
work	demonstrates	growing	feasibility	for	automated	speech	and	disfluency	analysis.	(
rcslt.org
)
The	project’s	novelty	lies	in	translating	known	clinical	predictors	into	an	automated,	interpretable,	child-level
risk-prediction	pipeline.	The	strongest	path	is	to	begin	with	an	interpretable	baseline,	obtain	longitudinal
pediatric	data	such	as	FluencyBank	IISRP,	validate	strictly	at	the	child	level,	and	position	the	tool	as	decision
support	for	speech-language	pathologists—not	as	an	autonomous	diagnostic	system.	(
arxiv.org
